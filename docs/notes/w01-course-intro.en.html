Course Introduction and Project Structure

Note: summarised from the class's automatic transcript (INT182_G2, 7 Aug 2026). The first stretch (roughly 0–7 minutes) is off-topic conversation and unclear audio from the automatic transcription and is not summarised. The summary begins where the lecturer introduces herself and explains the course (from 7:57 onwards).

The lecturers

  • Asst. Prof. Dr. Chakarida Nukoolkit (chakarida@sit.kmutt.ac.th) — the main lecturer for the first half (the transcript garbled the name; the spelling is confirmed from ../Syllabus-INT182-DS_AI-1-69.pdf)
  • Assoc. Prof. Dr. Pornchai Mongkolnam (pornchai@sit.kmutt.ac.th) — the lecturer for the second half

An overview of the course

  • The material splits into two equal parts: Data Science and Artificial Intelligence (AI)
  • The two overlap; they are not cleanly separable
  • What students should come away able to do: analyse a real business or scientific problem → work out what kind of solution it needs → choose the right technology for it

Attendance

  • First session: simply sign the attendance sheet
  • In later sessions Ajarn Pornchai will adapt how attendance is taken as appropriate — calling on people in class, or setting an exercise or piece of project work

Project structure

  • Work in groups of 4–5
  • Split into three phases (the transcript covers only phase 1 in this session)
    1. Phase 1: each team finds an idea for the solution it will build
  • You are not required to build and train every model yourself — calling a generative AI API as part of the solution is allowed
  • Teams that would rather build and train their own models may do that too

Advice about data (the data science side)

  • Choose a problem where the data is already available (any file format), so that less time goes into finding data and more into the project itself
  • The rough sequence discussed:
    1. Find a data science practice that answers a business need
    2. Get an initial understanding of the data through visualisation and data exploration (statistics may help)
    3. Modelling — which depends on the shape of the problem, for instance
      • Clustering: grouping the data and understanding how it arranges itself — the example given: splitting a customer base of 20,000 people into five groups by their characteristics
      • Predictive model: predicting an outcome from the data — the example given: a model helping diagnose whether a patient is likely to have colon cancer from their test results (the transcript cuts off mid-sentence)
